AI Policy Analysis

Analyzing 46 states across five data snapshots from 2018 to 2025, a clear pattern emerges: the post-pandemic attendance crisis was universal, but the recovery has been sharply unequal. States that bounced back fastest — Alabama (only 0.9 percentage points above its pre-pandemic baseline), Delaware (+2.1 pts), and Iowa (+3.8 pts) — share a policy thread: early-warning systems flagging students missing 3 or more days in the first month, ESSER-funded reengagement coordinators embedded in school buildings, and attendance enforcement modernized with proactive family outreach before penalties. By contrast, states still in deep crisis — Alaska (42.8%), Washington D.C. (39.5%), Oregon (33.5%), New Mexico (33.1%) — have fragmented attendance tracking and exhausted federal recovery funds without building durable infrastructure. The national estimate dropped from 28% at peak to 22% in 2024–25, but five states are now moving backwards: Mississippi (+3.2 pts year-over-year), New Mexico (+3.3 pts), Colorado (+0.7 pts), D.C. (+0.3 pts), and Oklahoma (+0.1 pts). The recovery is real — but uneven and not guaranteed.

Voices: What the Data and Research Show

Verified findings from government sources and education policy researchers

Chronic absenteeism rates remain historically elevated in 2024–25, with the national estimate at approximately 22% — nearly 50% higher than the pre-pandemic baseline of 15%. While most states have improved since the 2021–22 peak, a significant number of states show rates that have stalled or are rising again.
FutureEd Chronic Absenteeism Tracker · Georgetown University · 2025
Source ↗
States that experienced the fastest attendance recovery shared measurable policy commonalities: dedicated reengagement teams funded from ESSER III, early-warning systems flagging students missing 3 or more days in the first month, and proactive family outreach before absences became habitual. Alabama, Delaware, and Iowa — the three fastest-recovering states by 2024–25 — implemented at least two of these interventions at scale.
AEI Lingering Absence Report · American Enterprise Institute · 2024
Source ↗
Chronic absenteeism and academic achievement operate in a reinforcing cycle: students who miss more school fall behind, and students who fall behind are more likely to stay home. Breaking that cycle requires intervention before absence becomes habitual — typically within the first five days of unexcused absence in a school year.
Attendance Works · National Research Summary · attendanceworks.org
Source ↗
Tennessee reduced chronic absenteeism from 20.3% in 2021–22 to 18.3% in 2024–25 — a consistent, if modest, recovery across four years. But the state remains 5.2 percentage points above its pre-pandemic baseline of 13.1%, meaning roughly 30,000 additional Tennessee students are chronically absent today compared to 2018–19.
Analysis · Tennessee Dept. of Education data + FutureEd Tracker · 2025
